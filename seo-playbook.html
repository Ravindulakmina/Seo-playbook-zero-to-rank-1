Chapter 00

Why most SEO plans fail.

Here's the truth most SEO guides skip past: Google doesn't actually rank pages. It ranks answers to questions, from sources it trusts. Once you understand that, everything else changes.

A brand new website has zero trust. So when beginners try to rank for a tough keyword like "freelance web designer" on day one, they're asking Google to trust them with the most contested spot in the niche. Google won't. No amount of on-page tricks can fix a trust problem.

The right approach is what I call a trust ladder. You earn the right to rank for harder keywords by first owning easier ones nearby. Every low-difficulty page you rank deposits a little bit into your topical authority bank. When the balance is high enough, Google starts considering you for the big keywords.

This document walks through exactly how to build that balance.

Worked Example

Throughout this playbook, we'll follow "Alex" — a freelance web designer based in Colombo. He eventually wants to rank #1 for "freelance web designer" (very high difficulty, ~70+) and "freelance web designer Sri Lanka" (medium, ~30).

01
Phase 0 · Week Zero

Strategic foundation.
Before you touch any code.

You can't SEO your way out of a bad strategic position. Get these three things right first.

0.1   Pick a niche you can defend

"Freelance web designer" is too broad for a new site to ever own. You need to narrow until you find a wedge. There are three ways to narrow:

  • By industry — "freelance web designer for dentists," "for SaaS startups," "for restaurants"
  • By technology — "Webflow freelance designer," "Shopify freelance designer," "Framer specialist"
  • By location — "freelance web designer in Colombo," "in Western Province"

Alex picks his wedge: "Webflow designer for early-stage SaaS startups, based in Sri Lanka, serving global clients." The portfolio still serves broader clients, but the content strategy targets this wedge until authority is built. Then you expand outward.

0.2   Map your buyer's search behavior

Before keyword research, write down: who is the buyer, and what do they search at each stage?

Stage What they search Intent
Unaware"why is my SaaS landing page not converting"Informational
Problem-aware"how to redesign a SaaS website"Informational
Solution-aware"Webflow vs Framer for SaaS sites"Commercial
Vendor-aware"freelance Webflow designer for SaaS"Transactional
Decision"[Alex's name] portfolio"Branded

Most freelancers only build pages for the bottom two stages. That's exactly why they don't rank. You need to build for all five.

0.3   Study the SERP before you write anything

For every keyword you target, the top 10 results are your brief. Open the search results and write down:

  • What kind of pages rank? (lists, service pages, blog posts, directories, YouTube)
  • Average word count of the ranking pages
  • Common subtopics they all cover
  • What entities (people, companies, technologies) keep getting mentioned
  • What special features show up (AI Overview, People Also Ask, Map Pack, Video Carousel)
  • What the ranking pages don't do well — that's your opening

If the top 10 are all directory pages (Upwork, Fiverr, Toptal), a single freelancer's portfolio will struggle there. Pick a different keyword where individual sites can rank.

02
Phase 1

Keyword research
and the KD Ladder.

1.1   The KD Ladder — the heart of your question

Keyword Difficulty (KD) tracks how many strong backlinks the ranking pages have. A new site can't beat that on day one. The ladder solves it:

Rung 4 · KD 60–90
Head terms
"freelance web designer"
Rung 3 · KD 30–50
Mid-tail commercial
"hire freelance Webflow designer"
Rung 2 · KD 15–30
Long-tail commercial
"freelance Webflow designer for SaaS"
Rung 1 · KD 0–15
Long-tail informational
"how much does a Webflow site cost for a startup"

How it works: every time you rank a Rung 1 page, you earn a few backlinks, some user signals, and topical relevance for your domain. Those signals make Rung 2 pages easier to rank. Rung 2 pages reinforce Rung 3. The site climbs the ladder, one rung at a time.

Critical rule

All rungs must live in the same topical neighborhood. Ranking for "best banana bread recipe" does not help you rank for "freelance web designer." Topical authority is topic-specific.

1.2   Building the keyword universe

Use a layered approach. Don't just dump a tool's keyword list into a spreadsheet.

Layer 1 — Seed keywords (30 minutes, manual)

Brainstorm 20–40 seed terms covering services, technologies, problems, and outcomes your buyers care about. For Alex: "Webflow design," "SaaS landing page," "conversion rate optimization," "website redesign," "freelance designer rates."

Layer 2 — Tool expansion

Use Ahrefs, Semrush, or free tools (Google Keyword Planner, AnswerThePublic, Keywords Everywhere). For each seed, pull matching terms, related terms, question terms (who/what/why/how), and comparison terms (X vs Y).

Layer 3 — SERP mining

Click into "People Also Ask" boxes (they expand the more you click). Read "Related searches" at the bottom. Hunt Reddit, Quora, IndieHackers, and niche forums for raw user language. Try YouTube autocomplete for different intent.

Layer 4 — Competitor gaps

Take 3–5 sites already ranking in your wedge. Run them through a tool's "organic keywords" report. Filter for keywords they rank for that you don't. These are validated opportunities.

1.3   Score and prioritize

For each keyword, capture:

FieldWhy it matters
Search volumeHow big is the demand
Keyword DifficultyHow much effort it takes
Search intentWhat page type to build
Business value (1–5)How close to revenue
SERP featuresWhether clicks even reach pages
CPCProxy for commercial value
Top page authorityRealistic threshold to compete

Then compute a rough Priority Score:

Priority = (Volume × Business Value) / (KD × SERP feature dilution)

This surfaces high-leverage, low-effort terms. Tackle those first. Sort the whole keyword universe by priority — that's your content roadmap.

1.4   Classify search intent (don't skip this)

Every keyword maps to exactly one of four intents. The page type has to match:

  • Informational → blog post, guide, tutorial
  • Navigational → branded landing page or homepage
  • Commercial → comparison, listicle, review
  • Transactional → service page, pricing, contact

If you write a blog post for a transactional keyword, you won't rank — Google wants to show service pages there. Mismatched intent is the #1 on-page reason rankings fail.

Technical SEO doesn't make you rank. It removes the ceilings that would otherwise stop you from ranking.

03
Phase 2

Technical SEO foundation.

Get it right once. Audit it every quarter.

2.1   Crawlability and indexability

  • robots.txt: only block what genuinely shouldn't be crawled (cart pages, internal search, staging). Never block CSS or JS — Google needs them to render.
  • XML sitemap: auto-generated, segmented if large, submitted to Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools.
  • noindex audit: open GSC → Pages → "Excluded by 'noindex' tag." Anything important here is a bug.
  • Canonical tags: every indexable page gets a self-referencing canonical. All variants point to one version.
  • HTTP status codes: 200 for live pages, 301 for permanent moves, 404 for genuinely gone, 410 for intentionally removed-forever. Never 302 for permanent moves. Never soft-404s.

2.2   Site speed and Core Web Vitals

As of 2026, the three Core Web Vitals are LCP, INP, and CLS. Targets:

LCP
< 2.5s

Largest Contentful Paint. Usually your hero image. Preload it, serve WebP/AVIF, use a CDN.

INP
< 200ms

Interaction to Next Paint. Replaces FID. Cut main-thread JS, defer non-critical scripts.

CLS
< 0.1

Cumulative Layout Shift. Set width/height on images, reserve space for embeds.

Measure with PageSpeed Insights, then verify in real-user data via GSC's Core Web Vitals report (that's what Google actually uses).

2.3   Mobile first

Google indexes the mobile version of your site. Test on a real phone, not just devtools. Tap targets at least 48px, no horizontal scroll, no annoying interstitials, fonts at least 16px (smaller and iOS zooms).

2.4   Structured data (schema.org)

Schema doesn't directly improve rankings. It unlocks rich results that increase click-through rate — and CTR feeds back into rankings indirectly. For Alex's portfolio:

  • Person schema on the About page (name, jobTitle, sameAs links to LinkedIn / Twitter / Behance)
  • ProfessionalService or LocalBusiness on homepage and service pages
  • Article + BreadcrumbList on blog posts
  • FAQPage on pages with real, visible Q&A (Google penalizes hidden FAQ schema)
  • CreativeWork on portfolio case studies

Validate with the Rich Results Test and Schema.org validator.

2.5   Architecture and URL structure

  • URLs are short, lowercase, hyphenated, descriptive: /services/webflow-design — not /p?id=482
  • URL depth ≤ 3 levels for important pages
  • One canonical URL per topic. Don't fragment with category/tag pages
  • 301 any URL change. Never break a URL with external links pointing to it

2.6   JavaScript rendering

If your site is built with React, Vue, or Next, make sure pages are server-rendered or pre-rendered. Test in GSC's URL Inspection tool — view the rendered HTML and confirm your content is actually there. Client-side-only rendering is a top reason new sites' content stays invisible to Google.

2.7   Internal linking foundations

  • Every page reachable in 3 clicks or less from the homepage
  • No orphan pages (pages with zero internal links pointing to them)
  • Anchor text varied and descriptive — never "click here"
  • Strategically link from high-authority pages (homepage, top blog posts) toward your money pages

We'll exploit internal linking structurally in Phase 5.

2.8   Security and trust

  • HTTPS everywhere with a valid certificate, HSTS header
  • A real privacy policy and terms page (yes, Google looks)
  • A real contact page with real contact info
  • For freelance/service sites: visible address, phone, business registration where applicable
04
Phase 3

On-page SEO.

For every page you build, these are non-negotiable.

3.1   Title tag

  • Primary keyword near the front
  • Under ~60 characters to avoid truncation
  • Include a value modifier ("2026," "for SaaS," "with case studies")
  • Unique across the site
Freelance Webflow Designer for SaaS Startups | Alex Perera

3.2   Meta description

  • Doesn't directly rank, but drives click-through
  • Under ~155 characters
  • Include the primary keyword (Google bolds it in results)
  • Have a clear call to action

3.3   Headings and content structure

  • Exactly one H1, with the primary keyword (or close variant)
  • H2s for major sections, H3s nested under them
  • Headings follow real user intent, not keyword stuffing
  • Use the SERP's "People Also Ask" questions as your H2s and H3s — Google has already told you what users want

3.4   Content depth and entity coverage

Modern Google ranks based on entity coverage, not keyword density. For "freelance Webflow designer for SaaS," ranking pages will mention entities like: Webflow, CMS, conversion rate, landing page, A/B testing, B2B, ICP, MVP, design system, Figma. Map and cover the entities the ranking pages share.

Tools like Surfer, Frase, Clearscope, or NeuronWriter compute this for you. Or do it manually by reading the top 5 ranking pages and listing every distinct entity they cover.

3.5   Internal linking (per page)

  • Each new page links out to 3–8 related pages on your site
  • Each new page is linked to from at least 2–3 existing pages
  • Anchor text uses keyword variations, not exact match every time

3.6   Image SEO

  • Descriptive filenames (webflow-saas-redesign-before-after.webp, not IMG_4827.jpg)
  • Real alt text describing the image (also helps accessibility)
  • WebP or AVIF format, compressed
  • Width and height attributes set
  • Lazy-load below-the-fold images (loading="lazy")

3.7   The "above the fold" audit

The top 600 pixels of every page should answer: who, what, why-you. New visitors decide in 3 seconds whether to stay. Bounce rate is a ranking signal — through dwell-time inferences.

Site architecture

Here's the actual sitemap Alex builds:

/ (Homepage — brand + primary service) /about (E-E-A-T anchor) /services/ (Service hub) /services/webflow-design /services/saas-landing-pages /services/website-redesign /portfolio/ (Case study hub) /portfolio/[client-1] /portfolio/[client-2] ... /blog/ (Content hub) /blog/[topical-cluster-1]/ /blog/article-1 /blog/article-2 /blog/[topical-cluster-2]/ ... /locations/ (Local SEO, only if relevant) /locations/colombo /locations/sri-lanka /contact /privacy /terms

The hub-and-spoke structure is intentional: it concentrates internal link equity on the pages you most need to rank.

06
Phase 5

The topical authority
flywheel.

This is where the KD ladder actually gets executed.

5.1   The cluster model

For each money keyword (the high-KD term you ultimately want to rank for), build a pillar page + 8–20 supporting articles that all link to it.

Example cluster: "Webflow designer for SaaS"

Pillar (the page that will eventually rank for the head term):

/services/webflow-design-for-saas

Supporting spokes (each ranks for a long-tail Rung 1 or Rung 2 keyword):

1. How much does a Webflow site cost for a SaaS startup
2. Webflow vs Framer for B2B SaaS in 2026
3. How long to build a SaaS landing page in Webflow
4. SaaS landing page conversion rate benchmarks
5. Above-the-fold patterns for SaaS homepages
6. How to brief a freelance Webflow designer
7. Webflow CMS for SaaS blogs — setup guide
8. Migrating from WordPress to Webflow
9. Webflow client handoff checklist
10. Common Webflow design mistakes on SaaS sites
11. How to A/B test a Webflow landing page
12. SaaS website redesign: full process
13. Hiring an agency vs a freelancer
14. Webflow designer rates explained
15. Pre-launch SaaS website checklist

Each spoke:

  • Targets one specific long-tail keyword
  • Internally links to the pillar with varied anchor text
  • Links to 2–3 other spokes in the cluster

The pillar links out to the spokes. It doesn't need link juice from them in raw PageRank terms — what it gets is topical proof that this site covers the entire neighborhood.

5.2   Why this works mechanically

When Google evaluates a page for a query, it looks at three dimensions:

  1. 1Does this page match the query? (on-page relevance)
  2. 2Does the rest of the site demonstrate expertise on this topic? (topical authority)
  3. 3Do trustworthy external signals back this up? (backlinks, mentions, engagement)

Without a topical cluster, dimension #2 is empty. Dimension #1 alone won't beat established competitors. The cluster builds dimension #2 systematically.

5.3   Publishing cadence and depth

  • Quality threshold: every article ≥ 1,500 words is a minimum, not a goal. Match the depth of ranking competitors. Some informational queries need 3,000+ words. Some commercial pages convert better at 800.
  • Cadence: 2–4 high-quality articles per week if you're solo. Consistency beats volume — Google favors regularly updated sites. But thin or AI-generated filler is now actively penalized by the Helpful Content System.
  • Originality: include things only you can include. Real screenshots from real projects. Original data ("I analyzed 50 SaaS landing pages and found…"). Personal experience and opinions. This is the strongest signal of authentic expertise.

5.4   The climb — sequencing the ladder

M 1–2
Publish 8–15 Rung 1 articles (KD 0–15). Goal: first organic clicks, validate the topic, see what resonates.
M 3–5
Another 15–20 articles, mixing Rung 1 and Rung 2 (KD 15–30). Build out the cluster around the pillar. Internal-link aggressively.
M 4–6
Start light link-building (Phase 7). Begin updating early articles based on GSC query data — refresh, expand, republish.
M 6–9
Push into Rung 3 (KD 30–50). Publish the pillar pages now if not already live; they should benefit from all the cluster signal you've built.
M 9–12+
Target Rung 4 head terms with the pillars. By now you should have 50–80 ranking pages, real backlinks, brand searches, topical credibility. Head terms become reachable.

This is the climb, made concrete.

5.5   Content refresh — the underrated multiplier

Republishing wins. Every quarter:

  • Pull GSC's "queries" report for each page
  • For pages ranking #5–#20 for relevant queries, expand the page to cover those queries
  • Update statistics, screenshots, and the year in titles
  • Update the publication date when material changes happen
  • Resubmit to GSC

Refreshed content often outperforms new content because it inherits existing internal links and external signals.

07
Phase 6

E-E-A-T
for a portfolio site.

Experience · Expertise · Authoritativeness · Trust

Since Google's December 2022 update, E-E-A-T applies broadly — not just to "Your Money or Your Life" topics. For a service-business site like a freelancer's portfolio, weak E-E-A-T means weak rankings.

First E

Experience

First-person language: "When I built this for…" Real photos of real work, not stock. Behind-the-scenes content: process, tools, screenshots. Original data and case studies with metrics.

Second E

Expertise

Author bio on every article with credentials, photo, and links. A detailed /about page covering background, education, certifications, years of experience. Speaking engagements, podcast appearances, published articles elsewhere — list them. Person schema with sameAs linking to LinkedIn, GitHub, Behance, Dribbble.

A

Authoritativeness

Clients you've worked with (logos with permission). Testimonials with full names, companies, links to their LinkedIn. Press mentions, awards, certifications. A consistent publishing presence.

T

Trust

HTTPS, real privacy policy, real terms. Real contact information, ideally with a real address. Transparent pricing or pricing ranges. A genuine refund/guarantee policy where applicable. No deceptive design.

For a freelance portfolio specifically: your About page and case studies are your E-E-A-T workhorses. Don't treat them as afterthoughts. They should be among your most polished pages.

08
Phase 7

Off-page SEO
and link building.

Backlinks remain one of the strongest ranking signals. The best link is one a competitor can't easily replicate.

7.1   The link hierarchy (best to worst)

  1. Editorial links from authoritative niche sites, given because your content is genuinely useful
  2. Digital PR links — original research / data picked up by journalists
  3. Guest posts on respected niche publications
  4. Resource page inclusions
  5. Podcast appearance show notes
  6. Community contributions (genuine, not spammy)
  7. Niche directory listings (relevant ones only)
  8. Profile and citation links (for local SEO)
Avoid

PBNs, comment spam, link exchanges at scale, link farms, sponsored "do-follow" links without disclosure. Google's SpamBrain catches these and demotes the entire site, sometimes for good.

7.2   Tactics that actually work for a freelancer

  • Original research / data studies. "I analyzed 100 Y Combinator SaaS homepages — here's what 80% get wrong." Pitch to industry newsletters, designers on Twitter, niche communities. Highest-ROI link-building tactic for a solo operator.
  • HARO / Connectively / Featured / Help A B2B Writer. Respond to journalist queries in your area of expertise. Quoted = backlink + authority signal.
  • Podcast tour. Book yourself on 5–10 niche podcasts. Each episode produces a backlink and brand awareness.
  • Guest posts. Target 1–2 high-quality guest posts per month on niche publications. Pitch a real angle, not a generic post.
  • Tool / resource creation. Build a free tool (e.g., a "SaaS landing page audit checklist," a "Webflow pricing calculator"). Tools naturally attract links.
  • Community contribution. Be genuinely helpful in 2–3 communities (subreddits, IndieHackers, Designer News, niche Discords). Don't drop links — answer questions thoroughly. Links and brand searches follow naturally.

7.3   Velocity and naturalness

Avoid sudden bursts. A new site getting 100 backlinks in week one looks unnatural. A steady curve — say, 2–10 quality links per month for the first year — looks like genuine growth.

7.4   Brand mentions count even without links

Google detects unlinked mentions ("implied links") and weighs them. Showing up in industry conversations, even without a hyperlink, builds authority.

09
Phase 8

Local SEO.

If "freelance web designer Sri Lanka" matters to your business, local SEO is its own work.

  • Google Business Profile: claimed, fully completed, with categories, services, photos, and posts. Freelancers without a public storefront can register as a service-area business.
  • NAP consistency: Name, Address, Phone identical across every directory and citation
  • Local citations: industry-relevant local directories — be selective, quality over quantity
  • Local landing pages: /locations/colombo should be genuinely useful, not a thin "we serve [city]" template
  • Reviews: actively request from clients, respond to every one
  • LocalBusiness schema with areaServed markup for the regions you cover
  • Local backlinks: from local newspapers, business associations, universities

Phase 9

Measurement
and iteration.

You can't improve what you don't measure.

9.1   The five tools you actually need

  1. Google Search Console — the source of truth for what Google sees
  2. Google Analytics 4 — user behavior on-site
  3. Bing Webmaster Tools — secondary, but free data
  4. A rank tracker (Ahrefs, Semrush, SerpRobot) — daily position tracking
  5. A crawler (Screaming Frog free up to 500 URLs) — quarterly site audits

9.2   The dashboard

Track weekly
  • · Total clicks (GSC)
  • · Total impressions (GSC)
  • · Average position for tracked keywords
  • · Keywords on page 1, page 2, top 3
  • · New referring domains
  • · Core Web Vitals status
  • · Indexation count vs total pages
Track monthly
  • · Conversions (leads, calls, contacts)
  • · Conversion rate by landing page
  • · Revenue attributed to organic
  • · Top exit pages
  • · Bounce-heavy pages

9.3   The quarterly iteration loop

  1. 1.Identify striking-distance keywords — terms ranking #5–#20 with meaningful volume. The cheapest wins.
  2. 2.Identify the gap — content depth? Backlinks? Internal links? Technical issue?
  3. 3.Fix the gap — expand the article, point internal links at it, build a backlink, fix CWV.
  4. 4.Wait 2–6 weeks, measure, document what worked.

Most ranking gains come from this loop — not from publishing yet another new article.

Phase 10

The KD climb,
step by step.

Here, in order, is the move from low KD to high KD.

01
Pick the head term you ultimately want. For Alex: "freelance web designer."
02
Map the topical neighborhood. What 50–100 keywords surround it? Use clustering tools or manual SERP analysis.
03
Build the pillar page now — even though it won't rank yet. It exists so spokes have a target.
04
Publish 20–30 spoke articles targeting Rung 1 keywords (KD 0–15). Each links to the pillar and to 2–3 other spokes. Each shows real expertise.
05
Begin earning backlinks via Phase 7 tactics — especially to the strongest spokes (often the original-research ones).
06
As Rung 1 articles start ranking, mine GSC for adjacent queries they pick up — and build Rung 2 articles around those.
07
Target a few Rung 3 (KD 30–50) terms with carefully crafted commercial pages. By now the cluster has signal — these can rank within 2–6 months if competitors aren't dominant.
08
Refresh the pillar page with everything you've learned. Internal-link aggressively from the strongest spokes. Pitch it for a few high-value backlinks.
09
Wait. Head-term rankings often arrive 9–18 months after the cluster matures. Resist the urge to thrash.
10
When the pillar enters the top 30, optimize CTR (title, description, schema), build 3–5 high-quality backlinks pointing directly at it, refresh content. Usually enough to break into the top 10.
11
From #10 to #1, the work shifts from content to user-signal optimization. Make the page so good that users who land actually stay and convert. CTR, dwell time, and conversion data are the final mile.
Chapter 12

Common mistakes that stall sites.

  • Publishing without keyword research. "Whatever I felt like writing this week" rarely matches search demand.
  • Targeting head terms first. No new site ranks for "web designer" in month one. Start at the bottom.
  • Treating the blog as separate from money pages. The cluster strategy explicitly connects them.
  • Generic AI-generated content at scale. The Helpful Content System detects it. Use AI as a research/drafting assistant, not as the author.
  • Ignoring search intent. A blog post will not rank for a transactional query, no matter how well written.
  • Chasing every algorithm rumor. Fundamentals — useful content, technical hygiene, real authority — outlast every update.
  • Building links faster than you build content. Link velocity that outpaces content growth looks unnatural.
  • Not tracking which content actually converts. Ranking is a means to revenue, not the goal.
  • Abandoning the site before authority compounds. SEO timelines are 6–18 months. Most people quit at month 4.
  • Ignoring AI Overviews. In 2026 they appear on a large share of informational queries. To be cited: structured content, clear question-answer formatting, schema, and strong topical authority — the same things that make you rank.

Final note

What separates sites that
actually reach #1.

I've watched many sites execute the technical plan flawlessly and still stall. The difference is almost always one of three things.

  1. One

    Genuine differentiation. The ranking page actually says something the other pages don't. Original opinions, original data, original case studies.

  2. Two

    Real expertise visible on the page. A reader who lands on the article knows, within 30 seconds, that they're reading someone who actually does this work — not a content writer summarizing other articles.

  3. Three

    Persistence past the discouraging middle. The growth curve isn't linear. Months 4–7 often feel flat. Sites that ship through that period reach the inflection. Sites that quit don't.
